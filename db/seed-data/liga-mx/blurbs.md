# Liga MX — Club Blurbs (Apertura 2026)

### Club América
"Ódiame más." Hate me more. América wrote its own insult and turned it into a brand, which tells you most of what you need to know about the biggest, most televised, most accused-of-being-refereed club in Mexico. Sixteen league titles, and under André Jardine they did the thing nobody had managed in the short-tournament era: three straight, Apertura 2023 through Apertura 2024. Ownership runs through Televisa's orbit with Emilio Azcárraga Jean as the face of it, which is exactly why the rest of the country calls them plastic. After a stretch camped at Ciudad de los Deportes that their own fans admitted never felt like home, they're back at the Azteca, now the Estadio Banorte. Pick them if you want to win and don't mind being the reason people resent the league.

### Guadalajara (Chivas)
Mexican players only, forever, whatever it costs. Matías Almeyda put it plainly once: Chivas are always at a disadvantage against everyone else. The Rebaño Sagrado sells that handicap as virtue, alongside the claim to 40 million fans and the title of Mexico's most Mexican club. No league championship since Clausura 2017, though, and Amaury Vergara trends every single time América lifts something; the Akron has booed him while he stood on the pitch collecting a Femenil trophy. Toluca's 2025 double pulled level on 12 titles, which stings in Jalisco more than anyone there will say out loud.

### Cruz Azul
"Cruzazulear" is dead. Bury it. La Máquina took a tenth title in May 2026 when Rodolfo Rotondi scored in the 95th minute against Pumas, under an interim coach, and the joke that defined the club through a 23-year drought no longer lands. The ownership is the odd part: a real workers' cooperative, which fans genuinely love, except that its former boss Guillermo "Billy" Álvarez spent more than four years as a fugitive before his arrest in January 2025, with prosecutors alleging roughly 1.2 billion pesos was moved out of the cooperative between 2013 and 2020. La Sangre Azul showed up through all of it. Ten now, and the suffering has been reassigned elsewhere.

### Pumas UNAM
"Cómo no te voy a querer" — how could I not love you — sung by a crowd inside a stadium owned by a public university, because UNAM owns the club outright. No corporation, no network, no franchise fee. La Rebel began in 1998 with about fourteen students and is now enormous, beloved, and dogged by reports of violence in the stands. They reached the Clausura 2026 final on roughly half of Cruz Azul's payroll and lost it at the death, and haven't won a league since 2011. The club neutrals feel guiltiest about not supporting.

### Tigres UANL
"Nuevos ricos sin arraigo" — new money, no roots. That's the standard jab at a club funded by the cement multinational CEMEX, with a university deal running to 2056 and a trophy cabinet assembled largely by import, André-Pierre Gignac above all. Tigres fans don't bother arguing. They point at the Volcán, and at Libres y Lokos, who recorded a diss track called "Llora Monterrey" for no reason other than to twist the knife into Rayados. Runners-up in both 2025 finals to Toluca, the December one lost 9-8 on penalties. The best atmosphere in North America, paid for with cement.

### Monterrey
FEMSA money, Sergio Ramos, Sergio Canales, Anthony Martial — and 13th place in Clausura 2026. Rayados are what happens when a beverage conglomerate sets out to buy a superclub and nails the buying part. The Estadio BBVA, El Gigante de Acero, is one of the finest grounds in the Americas, and La Adicción fills it whether or not the front office deserves it. Matías Almeyda arrived in May 2026 to repair a side that missed the Liguilla entirely. The Clásico Regio is the reason to be here; the rest reads like a spreadsheet.

### Toluca
El infierno. The Nemesio Díez is small, loud, high, and visiting sides hate it — the Cumbia de los Trapos starts up and the Diablos Rojos score. After fifteen years with nothing, Toluca won Clausura 2025 and then Apertura 2025 under Antonio "El Turco" Mohamed, the second sealed by a 24-kick shootout against Tigres that Alexis Vega finished 9-8. Twelve titles now, level with Chivas. Owned quietly by the Díez family, without the ownership circus that follows almost everyone else in this league. The most entertaining team in Mexico and the least famous for it.

### Santos Laguna
"En las buenas todos, en las malas solo nosotros" — in good times everyone, in bad times only us. La Komún wrote that in 2001 and spent 2025-26 proving it, turning out in all black at the Estadio Corona to mourn a team that finished 18th and last, conceding 38 goals in 17 games. Alejandro Irarragorri stood up in February 2026 and said the responsibility was his, which the Comarca appreciated rather less than a sale would have. Orlegi sold Atlas and kept Santos, which in Torreón felt like being the child left behind. Support them for the barra, because the football is unwatchable right now.

### León
Thrown out of a World Cup they'd qualified for. FIFA expelled La Fiera from the 2025 Club World Cup because Grupo Pachuca owned them and Pachuca both, CAS upheld it, and the club called the sentence cruel and the rivals influential. Eight league titles and the 2023 CONCACAF Champions Cup say this is a serious institution; 7 wins, 9 defeats and 32 conceded in Clausura 2026 say it's currently a coin flip every week. Grupo Pachuca is selling a majority stake to end the multi-club problem, at a figure reported anywhere from around $100M to a $500M ask. A proper Bajío club with a real grievance and no defence.

### Pachuca
The Cuna del Futbol Mexicano — football landed here with Cornish miners more than a century ago, and the club has the Salón de la Fama and the Universidad del Futbol to prove it takes that seriously. Pachuca develops players and sells them, which is admirable and also means the good ones leave. They remain the only Mexican club to have won the Copa Sudamericana, back in 2006, and they put Toluca out 3-0 on aggregate to reach the Clausura 2026 semifinals. Tidy, methodical, rarely thrilling. The club you respect more than you enjoy.

### Atlas
Seventy years without a title, and La Fiel stayed. That's the identity: the curse of Tubo Gómez, La Barra 51 named for the 1951 championship, and then Apertura 2021 and Clausura 2022 back to back, which Guadalajara has still not fully processed. Canelo Álvarez and Peso Pluma are Atlas. So is scoring 16 goals in 17 games in Clausura 2026 and reaching the Liguilla anyway. Grupo Orlegi sold the club to Grupo PRODI in April 2026 for a reported $200-250M, finally ending the awkward shared ownership with Santos. Wonderful fanbase, dreadful attack.

### Necaxa
Eva Longoria, Ryan Reynolds, Rob McElhenney, and a docuseries. Los Rayos are Liga MX's Wrexham cosplay, a historic club with real 1990s silverware that now draws more international coverage for its cap table than for anything happening in Aguascalientes. The awkward bit predates Hollywood: Necaxa was moved out of Mexico City, and no amount of celebrity investment hands a relocated club its roots back. Fifteenth in Clausura 2026, 19 scored, 25 conceded. Great to follow online, thin on the ground.

### Puebla
La Franja, the diagonal stripe, one of the best shirts in the Americas worn by one of the worst teams. Puebla shipped seven to Tigres in August 2025 and managed 13 goals across the whole of Clausura 2026, which is less a slump than a condition. Ownership sits in the Grupo Salinas orbit, and the Camoteros have burned through coaches at a rate suggesting nobody involved has a plan. Los Malkriados keep turning up regardless. The shirt is the reason; at the moment there is no other reason.

### Querétaro
On 5 March 2022 home supporters crossed into the away section at the Corregidora and at least 26 people were injured. The match was abandoned, the fans were banned for a year, the ownership group was banned for five, and the club was forced into a sale — and that is still what Querétaro means to most of Mexico. Grupo Caliente held it in the interim before Innovatio Capital bought in for a reported $120M in July 2025, the first majority-U.S. ownership in Liga MX. On the pitch: the lowest payroll in the league, no quarterfinal since 2019, never a champion, and eight draws in 17 games last Clausura. A club trying very hard to become boring, which is probably the correct goal.

### FC Juárez
Twenty-six scored, thirty-two conceded, twelfth place. Bravos are the most watchable bad team in Mexico, which counts for something, and they play in the league's smallest ground, the Estadio Olímpico Benito Juárez, in front of crowds that sank to 6,053 in October 2025. Alejandra de la Vega Arizpe is Liga MX's first female club owner and has built the thing around civic pride in a city that badly needs some — "queremos crear héroes." It reached the top flight in 2019 by buying Lobos BUAP's place, which is the Liga MX way and still counts against it. Nobody will ever ask why you support them, because nobody will guess.

### Tijuana (Xolos)
Xolos belong to Jorge Hank Rhon: gambling magnate, former mayor, a man once detained after a reported 80-odd weapons were found at his home, and still genuinely popular among working-class Tijuanenses. The club is the most binational thing in North American football, a heavily Mexican-American squad and a support that crosses the line in both directions, with La Masakr3 inside the Estadio Caliente. They won the Apertura 2012 playing fast and nasty; Clausura 2026 was eight draws and ninth. A fan was shot dead in the stadium car park after a July 2024 match and Hank publicly called such things isolated incidents. Everything good here arrives with an asterisk you can see from orbit.

### Atlético San Luis
Atlético Madrid owns them and dresses them in red and white, and that is most of the story. San Luis is the competent satellite: sensible recruitment, the occasional Liguilla upset, and no reason for anyone outside San Luis Potosí to feel much. João Pedro Galvão scored 14 to finish Clausura 2026 as the league's top scorer for a side that came 14th. They've had the better of neighbours Puebla lately, which is the closest thing to a derby on offer. Liga MX with the volume turned down.

### Atlante
Los Potros de Hierro are back after twelve years away, and they got here by buying Mazatlán's licence for a reported $65M to $150M. Founded in 1916, one of the genuinely old Mexican clubs, restored by precisely the mechanism its own supporters spent a decade condemning. Mazatlán was itself Monarcas Morelia relocated in 2020 by the Grupo Salinas people, stripping Michoacán of its team just before the club's 70th anniversary, and it folded after six years having won nothing. Miguel "El Piojo" Herrera, an Atlante title-winner as a player in 1993, is in charge, and they'll share the Estadio Banorte with América. Great name, great history, ugly paperwork.
