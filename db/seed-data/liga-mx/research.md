# Liga MX Apertura 2026: Reputation, Fan Culture & Public Perception of All 18 Clubs

*Research brief for a "which club should you support" quiz. Current as of the Apertura 2026 tournament (kickoff July 16, 2026). Organized per club with reputational texture, ownership/ethics detail, barra culture, and recent events, plus scoring guidance across the four axes (Vibe, Play, Ethics, Fanbase).*

## TL;DR
- The confirmed Apertura 2026 lineup is 18 clubs, but with a major twist versus the brief's assumptions: **Mazatlán FC is dissolved and replaced by the returning Atlante**, which bought Mazatlán's league certificate; **promotion/relegation was ruled by CAS (Sept 4, 2025) to return for 2026-27** (though the FMF has since tried to keep it suspended, triggering a fresh appeal); the **play-in is permanently abolished**; and **Liga MX voted unanimously on April 23, 2026 to split from the FMF** and run as an independent civil association.
- Multi-club ownership is being dismantled fast: **Querétaro sold to Innovatio Capital for a reported $120M (July 2025)**, **Atlas sold by Grupo Orlegi to Grupo PRODI for a reported $200–250M (approved April 23, 2026)**, and **León is being sold by Grupo Pachuca**, leaving Orlegi (Santos) and Grupo Pachuca (Pachuca) as the last groups untangling.
- Recent champions frame current perceptions: **América's historic three-peat (Apertura 2023, Clausura 2024, Apertura 2024)** under André Jardine, **Toluca's bicampeonato (Clausura & Apertura 2025)** under Antonio Mohamed, and **Cruz Azul's 10th title, "La Décima" (Clausura 2026)** — which, combined with their 2021 crown, has largely retired the "cruzazulear" choke joke.

## Key Findings

**League structure has been transformed.** The 2026-27 season is the 80th of Liga MX. On April 23, 2026 the 18 owners voted unanimously to make Liga MX an independent Asociación Civil with four new committees (Deportivo; Comercial; Inversión y Certificación; Ética y Buen Gobierno), a governance model borrowed from Europe, effective with the July 16, 2026 Apertura kickoff. The play-in round (active since Apertura 2023) was permanently eliminated; the top eight teams now advance directly to the quarterfinals. Promotion/relegation, frozen since 2020, was ordered by the Court of Arbitration for Sport (ruling Sept 4, 2025) to return for 2026-27 — but the FMF's July 2026 rulebook (Art. 35) kept it suspended, prompting a renewed CAS challenge, so its true status is unsettled.

**The 18 clubs for Apertura 2026:** América, Guadalajara (Chivas), Cruz Azul, Pumas UNAM, Tigres UANL, Monterrey, Toluca, Santos Laguna, León, Pachuca, Atlas, Necaxa, Puebla, Querétaro, FC Juárez, Tijuana (Xolos), Atlético San Luis, and **Atlante** (replacing the dissolved Mazatlán).

**Multi-ownership is collapsing.** Grupo Caliente sold Querétaro to Atlanta-based Innovatio Capital (Marc Spiegel) for a reported $120M, closed July 11, 2025 — the first majority-U.S. ownership in Liga MX — keeping only Xolos. Grupo Orlegi sold Atlas to Grupo PRODI (José Miguel Bejos) for a reported $200–250M, approved at the FMF Owners' Assembly on April 23, 2026, with control transferring July 1, 2026 (Orlegi retains Santos). Grupo Pachuca announced the sale of a majority stake in León to comply with the 2026 deadline, after León was infamously expelled from the 2025 FIFA Club World Cup because it shared ownership with Pachuca.

## Details — Club by Club

### Club América (Águilas) — Mexico City
The villain everyone loves to hate — the single most important reputational archetype in Liga MX. América is Mexican football's establishment giant, owned via Televisa/Grupo Ollamani with Emilio Azcárraga Jean as the face of ownership. Rivals see them as the TV-network favorite: the arrogant, referee-favored, "plastic" glory club. América fans embrace the villainy with the famous **"Ódiame más"** ("hate me more") ethos and the "ley del más grande." On the pitch they just completed the greatest modern dynasty — the **first-ever three-peat of the short-tournament era** (Apertura 2023, Clausura 2024, Apertura 2024) under André Jardine, who went unbeaten in Liguilla series before leaving in June 2026 after six titles in three years (replaced by Guillermo Almada). Record 16-time champions. Barra: **La Monumental / Ritual del Kaoz**. Rivalries: Clásico Nacional vs Chivas, Clásico Joven vs Cruz Azul, Clásico Capitalino vs Pumas. América was displaced from the Estadio Azteca during its renovation (playing at Estadio Ciudad de los Deportes, where its own fans admitted it "never felt like home") and returns for Apertura 2026 to the renamed Estadio Banorte.

### Guadalajara (Chivas) — Guadalajara, Jalisco
The nationalist standard-bearer, the "Rebaño Sagrado" (Sacred Flock), famous for its Mexican-players-only policy — a source of enormous popularity and moral pride but a self-imposed competitive handicap ("siempre en desventaja con los demás equipos," as Matías Almeyda put it). Owned by Amaury Vergara (son of the late Jorge Vergara) through Grupo Omnilife. The dominant running joke is the title drought — no league title since Clausura 2017 — and a relentless coaching carousel. Fans routinely turn on Vergara, making him a trending-topic target after each América title, and have booed him even while he collected a Liga MX Femenil trophy at the Estadio Akron. Self-image tied to being "the club with 40 million fans" and Mexico's most authentically Mexican team. Now tied with Toluca on 12 league titles (a fact that stings after Toluca's 2025 double). Barras: Legión 1908, Irreverente. Rivalries: Clásico Nacional vs América, Clásico Tapatío vs Atlas.

### Cruz Azul (La Máquina) — Mexico City
The long-suffering faithful whose defining trait was **"cruzazulear"** — snatching defeat from the jaws of victory — a reputation built over a ~23-year drought broken in 2021. That joke is now largely buried: Cruz Azul won the **Clausura 2026, their 10th title ("La Décima")**, when Rodolfo Rotondi scored in the 95th minute for a 2-1 win over Pumas (2-1 aggregate; 0-0 first leg) at the Estadio Olímpico Universitario on May 24, 2026, under interim coach Joel Huiqui — ending a five-year wait. Ownership is a genuine workers' cooperative (Cooperativa La Cruz Azul), which fans view as authentically community-rooted, but it is deeply scandal-tainted: former boss **Guillermo "Billy" Álvarez**, a fugitive for over four years, was arrested January 16, 2025 in southern Mexico City; the FGR investigation cites roughly 1.2 billion pesos (~US$60.6M) transferred from the cooperative between 2013 and 2020 to offshore accounts, plus a separate warrant for buying 91.78M pesos in fake tax receipts. A related power struggle over the cooperative left eight people dead in a 2022 confrontation in Tula, Hidalgo. Barra: La Sangre Azul. Rivalry: Clásico Joven vs América.

### Pumas UNAM — Mexico City
The university club — literally owned by the Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México — carrying a student/intellectual, community-rooted, non-corporate identity that neutrals frequently find the most "likeable" and ethically clean in the league. Their anthem **"Cómo no te voy a querer"** ("si mi sangre es azul y mi piel dorada") and the barra **La Rebel** (founded 1998 by ~14 students, "La Banda del Pebetero") are among the league's most iconic traditions. Long trophy drought (last title Clausura 2011) but they reached the Clausura 2026 final, losing to Cruz Azul despite spending roughly half of Cruz Azul's squad budget. La Rebel has an ambivalent reputation — beloved and huge, but marked by episodes of violence and, more recently, allegations of drug-dealing activity in the stands.

### Tigres UANL — San Nicolás de los Garza, Nuevo León
The new-money superpower. Backed by cement multinational CEMEX (via Sinergia Deportiva, with the UANL contract extended to 2056) and nominally tied to the state university, Tigres built a dynasty by importing stars — above all French striker André-Pierre Gignac, a bona fide legend and the club's all-time top scorer. Rivals sneer at them as **"nuevos ricos sin arraigo"** (new rich without roots) with "no history," a jab even echoed by media columnists (e.g. José Ramón Fernández's "Tigres y Monterrey, nuevos ricos sin arraigo nacional") and by rivals mocking their wealth ("¡¡Ratones!!"). There are longstanding reports that CEMEX/FEMSA never paid UANL its promised profit share or stadium fees. Their fans counter with the incomparable **Volcán** atmosphere (Estadio Universitario) and the **Libres y Lokos** barra (founded 1998, ~3,000 members), who released a diss track "Llora Monterrey" to taunt Rayados over their historic relegation. Runners-up in both 2025 finals to Toluca (losing the Apertura 2025 final 9-8 on penalties). Rivalry: the fierce Clásico Regio vs Monterrey, widely called Mexico's most intense derby.

### Monterrey (Rayados) — Guadalupe, Nuevo León
The corporate-wealth counterpart to Tigres, owned by beverage giant FEMSA since 1999. Perceived as a big-spending, star-studded, front-office-driven club (recent marquee signings included Sergio Ramos, Sergio Canales, Lucas Ocampos, Anthony Martial). They play at the Estadio BBVA, **"El Gigante de Acero"** (Steel Giant), a nod to Monterrey's industrial heritage. Barra: La Adicción. Matías Almeyda ("El Pelado") was hired as coach in May 2026 (announced May 21) on a two-year deal after Rayados failed to reach the Liguilla. Rivalry: Clásico Regio vs Tigres.

### Toluca (Diablos Rojos) — Toluca, Estado de México
The provincial-consistency machine, owned by the Díez/Valdés family lineage (Grupo Valores; owner listed as Valentín Díez Morodo). After a 15-year drought they roared back as **back-to-back champions — Clausura 2025 and Apertura 2025** — under Antonio "El Turco" Mohamed, the bicampeonato sealed December 15, 2025 in an epic 9-8 penalty shootout over Tigres (24 kicks taken; Alexis Vega scored the decisive penalty against Nahuel Guzmán). Twelve league titles now tie them with Chivas; Mohamed became a champion with four different Mexican clubs. Their Estadio Nemesio Díez ("La Bombonera"/"el infierno") is a genuine home-field weapon; anthem "La Cumbia de los Trapos." Star: Portuguese scorer Paulinho. Qualified for the FIFA Intercontinental Cup in Qatar.

### Santos Laguna (Guerreros) — Torreón, Coahuila
The Comarca Lagunera's regional pride, owned by Grupo Orlegi (Alejandro Irarragorri; son Aleco named president in 2024). Once a model modern club, Santos is now in the deepest slump of its history, finishing dead last (sotanero) in the general table across multiple recent tournaments including Clausura 2026 (18th). Irarragorri publicly took the blame in February 2026 ("El máximo responsable... soy yo"). His fiscal-fraud legal case was closed with a no-grounds ruling in October 2025. Barra: **La Komún** (founded 2001; slogan "En las buenas todos, en las malas solo nosotros"), praised for loyalty amid the collapse and staging an all-black "mourning" protest at the Estadio Corona in 2026. Fans have demanded a sale.

### León (La Fiera) — León, Guanajuato
The Bajío's proud "Fiera," historically a competitive, well-run side (2023 CONCACAF Champions Cup winners, eight-time league champions). Their 2025-26 reputation is defined by grievance: **expelled from the 2025 FIFA Club World Cup** for sharing Grupo Pachuca ownership with Pachuca, a CAS-upheld ruling the club condemned as "cruel" and "unfair" ("The sentence was harsh, the rivals very influential"). Grupo Pachuca (Jesús Martínez) is now selling a majority stake (~51-60%) to end multi-ownership, with a valuation variously reported from ~$100M to a $500M asking price. Rivalry: regional Bajío clashes.

### Pachuca (Tuzos) — Pachuca, Hidalgo
The **"Cuna del Futbol Mexicano"** (cradle of Mexican football), the country's oldest club — introduced by British/Cornish miners, with a traditional 1901 founding (centenary celebrated 2001) and a revised club claim dating football to 1892. Owned by Grupo Pachuca (Martínez family), it is a serial developer and seller of talent and a continental overachiever — the only Mexican club to win the Copa Sudamericana (2006). Played in the 2025 FIFA Club World Cup and faced Real Madrid in the December 2024 Intercontinental Cup. Identity rooted in its mining heritage, its football-history mystique, and infrastructure like the Universidad del Futbol and the Salón de la Fama.

### Atlas (Rojinegros) — Guadalajara, Jalisco
The eternal long-suffering **"Fiel"** fanbase, famous for staying loyal through a **70-year title drought** finally broken with the Apertura 2021 title (plus the Clausura 2022 bicampeonato), ending the legendary "curse of Tubo Gómez." Barra: **La Barra 51**, named for the 1951 championship year. Beloved of Guadalajara celebrities (Canelo Álvarez, Peso Pluma). Sold by Grupo Orlegi to Grupo PRODI (José Miguel Bejos) for a reported $200–250M, approved April 23, 2026, ending its multi-ownership with Santos. Rivalry: Clásico Tapatío vs Chivas. On-field, though, a return to form remains elusive: finished 17th of 18 in both 2025-26 tournaments.

### Necaxa (Rayos) — Aguascalientes
The Hollywood-investor curiosity. A historic club with 1990s glory and multiple titles, relocated from Mexico City to Aguascalientes, now co-owned by a celebrity group including Eva Longoria and Wrexham owners Ryan Reynolds and Rob McElhenney, plus (at various points) Kate Upton, Odell Beckham Jr., Justin Verlander, Mesut Özil and others — drawing constant "next Wrexham" comparisons and a Necaxa docuseries. Perceived as a mid-table club with an outsized international/celebrity profile relative to its on-pitch results. Ethics note: the celebrity model is novel but the underlying franchise-relocation history (out of the capital) counts against community-rootedness.

### Puebla (La Franja / Camoteros) — Puebla
The historic-but-struggling giant, defined by its iconic diagonal-stripe **"La Franja"** shirt and the "Camoteros" nickname. Owned within the Grupo Salinas orbit (TV Azteca connections). Perceived by neutrals as a well-supported but chronically underachieving club; a rough 2025-26 (coaching changes; conceded a 7-0 to Tigres and multiple heavy losses). Barra: Malkriados.

### Querétaro (Gallos Blancos) — Querétaro
The club living under the shadow of the **2022 Corregidora riot** — one of the darkest days in Mexican football, when home supporters invaded the away section on March 5, 2022, leaving at least 26 injured (with disputed, unconfirmed death reports) and the match abandoned. The league forfeited the game 0-3, banned fans for a year, banned the ownership group from FMF-affiliated clubs for five years, and forced a sale. Grupo Caliente ran it in the interim before selling to Innovatio Capital (Marc Spiegel) for a reported $120M, closed July 11, 2025 — the first majority-U.S. ownership in Liga MX. Reputation: the league's lowest-payroll club, without a quarterfinal since 2019, and never a league champion.

### FC Juárez (Bravos) — Ciudad Juárez, Chihuahua
The border-city community club owned by Alejandra de la Vega Arizpe, Liga MX's first female club owner. Founded in 2015, it reached Liga MX in 2019 by buying Lobos BUAP's franchise, and frames itself as a source of civic pride in a city long scarred by extreme violence ("Queremos crear héroes"). Perceived as small and "irrelevant," playing in the league's smallest stadium (Estadio Olímpico Benito Juárez, ~19,700) with the league's lowest attendances (a 2025 game drew ~6,053). Barra: El Kartel, which has been involved in violent clashes.

### Tijuana (Xolos) — Tijuana, Baja California
The border-city, binational fanbase club, owned by Grupo Caliente / Jorge Hank Rhon — a controversial gambling magnate and former PRI mayor of Tijuana, previously detained after ~80 weapons were found at his home and long dogged by allegations of illegality and narco links, yet genuinely popular among working-class Tijuanenses. The 2012 Apertura champions built a fast, belligerent identity with a heavily Mexican-American squad; their barra **La Masakr3** and the "raza canina"/"Los Xolos" identity define a distinctly Tijuanense, cross-border pride. A fan was shot dead in the Estadio Caliente parking lot after a July 2024 match vs Chivas, with Hank controversially deflecting club responsibility ("hechos aislados").

### Atlético San Luis (Atleti) — San Luis Potosí
The Atlético de Madrid satellite, owned by the Spanish club, wearing red-and-white in homage (with blue-and-gold from the city crest). Perceived as a well-run but "soulless" farm-club satellite, though it has punched above its weight in recent Liguillas (a 2018 second-division final; upsetting bigger clubs) and holds a recent edge over neighbor-rival Puebla. Home: Estadio Alfonso Lastras.

### Atlante (Potros de Hierro) — returning to Liga MX
The nostalgic comeback story, owned by businessman Emilio Escalante. One of Mexico's oldest and most historic clubs (founded 1916), back after a 12-year absence — but by **buying dissolved Mazatlán's league certificate** (Owners' Assembly approval December 9, 2025; reported price ~$65M–$150M), a franchise purchase rather than a sporting promotion that itself embodies everything critics dislike about Liga MX's franchise model. Coached by Miguel "El Piojo" Herrera, an Atlante title-winner as a player in 1993. Its return is inseparable from Mazatlán's death: Mazatlán FC was itself the soulless 2020 relocation of Monarcas Morelia by TV Azteca/Grupo Salinas — condemned as a betrayal of Morelia's fans, leaving Michoacán without a team just before Monarcas' 70th anniversary — and is now dissolved after just six years and virtually no sporting success. Atlante will share the Estadio Banorte (Azteca) with América.

## Recommendations (for the quiz scoring)
- **Vibe (glamour/success/bigness):** Highest — América, Tigres, Monterrey, Cruz Azul. Mid — Chivas, Toluca (surging after the 2025 double), Pumas, Pachuca, León. Low — Querétaro, Juárez, San Luis, Necaxa, Atlante, and the fallen Santos.
- **Play (entertainment/chaos):** Reward Toluca (high-scoring, dramatic penalty finals), Cruz Azul, and América for consistent Liguilla drama; treat Santos' current chaos as *losing* chaos, not entertaining chaos. Benchmark that would change this: if Santos' rebuild under Renato Paiva lifts them out of the relegation zone, upgrade.
- **Ethics (ownership provenance):** Best/community-rooted — Pumas (university-owned, cleanest), Atlas (deep fan loyalty), and arguably Cruz Azul (worker cooperative, but heavily scandal-tainted by the Billy Álvarez fraud). Worst/most corporate or compromised — América (Televisa), Tigres (CEMEX), Monterrey (FEMSA), Tijuana and (historically) Querétaro (gambling money), Puebla and Mazatlán→Atlante (relocations/TV Azteca). **Penalize franchise relocations and franchise-purchases heavily** (Mazatlán→Atlante, Necaxa's move to Aguascalientes). Note the *improving* trajectory: the forced unwinding of multi-club ownership (Querétaro, Atlas, León) is a net ethics-positive story for the league.
- **Fanbase (intensity/atmosphere):** Elite — Tigres (the Volcán), Monterrey (La Adicción), Pumas (La Rebel), Atlas (La Fiel), América (La Monumental). Strong regional — Santos (La Komún's loyalty in adversity), Xolos (La Masakr3), León, Chivas (huge but frustrated). Weakest — Juárez, San Luis, Querétaro (riot-scarred), Necaxa, and Atlante (rebuilding a base after 12 years away).

## Caveats
- **Promotion/relegation status is genuinely unsettled.** CAS ruled (Sept 4, 2025) it must return for 2026-27, but the FMF's July 2026 rulebook reportedly kept it suspended (Art. 35), triggering a fresh appeal. Do not state flatly that relegation is back for this season.
- **Monterrey leadership:** "Tato" Noriega, named in the source brief as club president, had departed after Clausura 2026; Dennis te Kloese was reported as his replacement. Verify before publishing any president reference.
- **Cruz Azul's Apertura 2026 home venue is contested** in sources — some say the Estadio Banorte (Azteca), others say it stayed at Estadio Ciudad de los Deportes over a dispute with Grupo Ollamani.
- **Pachuca founding date** is cited as both 1901 (traditional/centenary) and 1892 (revised club research) — use "over a century old, Mexico's oldest club."
- **Santos barra:** confirmed groups are La Komún (main) and La Tribu; a group called "Guardia" could not be confirmed.
- **Estadio Azteca** was renamed **Estadio Banorte** in 2025 (Banorte lent ~2.1 billion pesos / ~$105M); during the World Cup it will be called "Estadio Ciudad de México" per FIFA sponsorship rules, and it hosts the **June 11, 2026 opener (Mexico vs South Africa)**, becoming the first stadium to host three World Cup opening/final events.
- **Valuations are approximate and sometimes single-sourced or reflect asking prices** (León ~$100M–$500M; Atlas $200–250M; Querétaro ~$120M; Atlante certificate ~$65M–$150M). Treat as ranges.
- **FC Juárez current sporting leadership** could not be reliably confirmed (some database entries appear stale); verify the coach/sporting director before publishing specifics.
- América's coaching change (Jardine out June 2026, Guillermo Almada in) and several 2026 coaching appointments are recent; confirm any that will appear as fixed facts.
